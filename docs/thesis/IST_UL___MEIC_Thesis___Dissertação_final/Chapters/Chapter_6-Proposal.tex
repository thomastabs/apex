\fancychapter{Research Proposal}
\label{chap:proposal}

This chapter presents the second and third activities of the \ac{DSRM}: the definition of objectives for a solution to the research problem formalised in \Cref{chap:problem}, and the design of the artefact that satisfies them, a process-oriented framework for governed Human-AI collaboration across the \ac{SDLC}. The framework is described here as a methodology, independently of any implementation; its instantiation as a working system is the subject of \Cref{chap:apex}. This design-and-development activity addresses the first two dimensions of that problem: \Cref{sec:phases} fixes where AI participates in each \ac{SDLC} phase and where its participation ends, and \Cref{sec:gates,sec:metrics} define the gates, metrics and human-in-the-loop practices that keep AI contributions traceable, validated and accountable. \Cref{sec:overview} introduces the vocabulary the rest of the chapter depends on before the detail follows: who performs the work (\Cref{sec:roles}), when (\Cref{sec:phases}), how it is governed (\Cref{sec:governance}), how it is performed day to day (\Cref{sec:playbooks}), and why sprints are replaced (\Cref{sec:work_organisation}).

\section{Purpose and Scope}
\label{sec:framework_scope}

% Framework doc §1. State plainly what the framework covers and what it does
% not. The exclusions matter: they pre-empt the "why is this not MLOps?"
% question from the committee.

The artefact is a method in the sense the \ac{DSRM} gives that term: a set of lifecycle phases, the responsibilities attached to each, the artefacts each must produce, and the rules for passing from one to the next; the vocabulary is introduced in \Cref{sec:overview}. It is stated at the level of process rather than of tooling, so that it can be assessed independently of the system instantiating it in \Cref{chap:apex} and adopted against a different tool stack. It covers AI-assisted activity in every phase rather than the code-authoring phases alone, the collaboration and model-selection practices that activity requires, expressed as process obligations rather than a catalogue of prompts, and the governance mechanisms of \Cref{sec:governance}.

It excludes the training or fine-tuning of \acp{LLM}, model architecture design, \ac{MLOps} pipelines and tool-specific detail. These are boundaries rather than omissions, following from \Cref{chap:problem}'s concern with teams that \emph{consume} general-purpose models rather than \emph{produce} them: \ac{MLOps} governs a model's lifecycle as a deployed asset, while this framework governs the lifecycle of software a model helped write, and a pipeline can be mature under the first while the second remains ungoverned, which is the deficiency \Cref{chap:slr} reports. Tool-specific detail is excluded for the same reason \Cref{chap:apex,chap:evaluation} are kept separate: a framework prescribing a vendor cannot be evaluated independently of it.

\section{Framework Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

% Added 2026-10-02: the vocabulary glossary the chapter previously lacked,
% see the file header for why. Every term defined here recurs, in detail,
% in a section cited inline.

The framework's most direct precedent is the \ac{AI-DLC} published by Amazon Web Services~\cite{AWS:2025aidlc}, which organises work into Inception, Construction and Operations. Three of its terms are adopted here unchanged and recur throughout this chapter: a \textbf{Bolt}, a short execution cycle measured in hours or days that replaces the sprint; a \textbf{Unit of Work}, the top-level grouping of business justification and acceptance criteria, optionally organised into Epics rather than replacing them as \ac{AI-DLC} does; and \textbf{Mob Elaboration}, the collaborative ritual in which the Squad critiques AI-drafted requirements before they are locked. No originality is claimed for any of the three, and \ac{AI-DLC}'s own reported ten-to-fifteen-fold productivity improvement is vendor-reported, unreplicated, and is not carried here as evidence; only its process vocabulary is adopted.

The team that receives a Unit of Work is the \textbf{Squad}. Every responsibility within it is a \textbf{hat}: a function the framework requires rather than a position that must be staffed by a dedicated person, so one individual may hold several and a small team may still leave none unfilled. Three hats, \ac{PO}, Tech Lead and Design Lead, jointly form the \textbf{Trio}, which owns Discovery and Design together; the full set of hats and the gate each owns is detailed in \Cref{sec:roles}.

Work is organised in a four-level hierarchy. A \textbf{Unit of Work} decomposes into \textbf{User Stories}, sized extra-small or small, each the minimum unit of observable business value and the unit \ac{QA} validates; a story decomposes into \textbf{Tasks}, granular technical steps queued in the \textbf{Bolt Backlog}; and a Bolt is the execution of one task. There are no sprints: tasks are pulled continuously rather than committed to in batches, an argument made in full in \Cref{sec:work_organisation}. Unlike \ac{AI-DLC}, which replaces the Epic outright, the framework leaves it optional: a team may still group a Unit of Work's stories into Epics, a project-management-tool-native sub-grouping the framework neither requires nor prohibits, and Epic-level organisation is independently supported by evidence of higher correctness and completeness than requirement-level decomposition~\cite{Siddeeq:2026gh}. The demonstration in \Cref{chap:demonstration} uses Epics throughout for exactly this reason.

Discovery produces \textbf{Gherkin} acceptance criteria, structured Given/When/Then scenarios the Trio approves before anything is built. Approved scenarios, together with the technical specification agreed at the Design Gate, are \textbf{anchored}: written into a persistent, versioned specification split into a project-wide layer and a per-story layer, detailed in \Cref{sec:spec_anchored}. Every phase that follows works against that anchor rather than against the whole project.

Each phase is bounded by a \textbf{quality gate}: a named owning hat, a specific body of required evidence, and a defined consequence when that evidence is absent. Five gates and three governance metrics, detailed in \Cref{sec:gates,sec:metrics}, are what the framework uses to decide whether a phase may be exited.

\section{Roles and Responsibilities}
\label{sec:roles}

% Framework doc §6. The "hats, not people" framing is a deliberate design
% decision and should be argued as one, not merely asserted.

Every role in the framework is a hat: a required function rather than a position that must be staffed by a dedicated person. One individual may therefore hold several hats, and on small teams responsibilities may be absorbed by adjacent functions. \Cref{tab:hats} summarises the hats and the gates they own. Expressing responsibility functionally rather than organisationally serves three purposes: it supports solo and small-team use, preserves accountability when formal titles are absent, and avoids treating structural models as directly transferable blueprints.

The Trio (\Cref{sec:overview}) draws inspiration from the Spotify Model, but not as a prescriptive template. Spotify's own account was descriptive rather than normative~\cite{Kniberg:2012sp}, later commentary explicitly framed it as inspiration rather than a blueprint~\cite{LeadingComplexity:2023sp}, and first-hand retrospective evidence identified unresolved matrix authority and autonomy-without-accountability problems~\cite{Lee:2020sq}. The framework addresses these through the Tech Lead's architectural lock (\Cref{sec:bolts}) and by separating Bolt logistics from technical authority, with each gate owned by a named function. Team Topologies provides a closer structural analogue through its emphasis on cognitive load~\cite{Skelton:2019tt}, while the \ac{PM} hat resembles Amazon's single-threaded leadership model~\cite{Bryar:2021wb}.

Deployment approval and infrastructure-security review remain mandatory but are owned by whoever performs the deployment, whether self-reviewed in solo work or peer-reviewed in a larger team. This avoids introducing governance bodies that the process assumes exist but that may never be staffed.

The gates themselves do not weaken as team size decreases. In solo work they are self-checked, but the required evidence in \Cref{tab:gates} must still exist, be recorded and be reviewed explicitly at the gate. Their value lies in enforcing a distinct validation step against a specification that predates the artefact, preserving meaningful review even when author and reviewer are the same person.

\section{Lifecycle Phases and the AI Interaction Mapping}
\label{sec:phases}

This section addresses the placement dimension of the problem. The framework organises the lifecycle into six phases, preceded by a Strategic Alignment stage that sits outside the lifecycle proper because it is performed before the Squad is engaged at all: it decides whether a Unit of Work should exist, whereas the six phases decide how one is built. The six are Discovery and Requirements; Design, Prototyping and Architecture; Implementation; Testing; Deployment; and Maintenance. \Cref{tab:phase_mapping} fixes, for the stage and each of them, what enters, what the model is permitted to produce, what leaves, which hat is accountable, and which control must be satisfied before exit. It is the central artefact of this chapter and is not restated in prose.

The phases are listed in an order, but that order is not the shape of the process: Maintenance neither terminates the lifecycle nor patches in place. Its exit is always an entry into a named earlier phase, whose gate then applies in full: a business deviation routes to the \ac{PM} hat for a value decision and, if approved, re-opens Discovery and Requirements; a specification gap re-opens Design, Prototyping and Architecture with the technical specification amended and re-versioned; a production defect re-enters Implementation as a Fix-Bolt and returns through Testing. This is not Waterfall with a maintenance tail: in Waterfall, feedback is measured against a specification treated as frozen, whereas here the specification is exactly what is re-opened, re-approved and re-versioned, and code is regenerated from it. What the framework refuses is the unnamed loop, a change applied to production code with no corresponding change to the specification it is supposed to satisfy, since that is how traceability is lost in practice. \Cref{fig:lifecycle} shows the six phases and these three loop-backs; \Cref{tab:phase_mapping} is the detail behind each box.

The Discovery and Requirements row names the mechanism behind that validation, the \textbf{Interactive Bridge}: the model drafts scenarios in natural language, and the Trio confirms, adds, deletes or modifies them before approved scenarios are compiled into Gherkin acceptance criteria (\Cref{sec:overview}) and anchored into the specification (\Cref{sec:spec_anchored}). Separating critique from formalisation this way lets domain experts catch an incorrect assumption without first having to read it through formal syntax. The same row also names \textbf{Mob Elaboration}, the Trio's structured session critiquing AI-drafted stories as a group before any are approved; the procedure is given in full as one of the six playbooks in \Cref{sec:playbooks}. The table's other rows name mechanisms the same way, the gates, the architectural lock, the \ac{QA} Validation Playbook among them, each unpacked in turn in \Cref{sec:governance,sec:playbooks} rather than here.

\textbf{Where the phase model departs from \ac{AI-DLC}.} Most of the expansion from three phases to six is decomposition rather than disagreement: Discovery and Requirements together with Design, Prototyping and Architecture occupy the territory \ac{AI-DLC} calls Inception, and Implementation corresponds to Construction. Two substantive extensions distinguish the proposed framework from it:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.6cm, itemsep=0.5em]

\item \textbf{Decoupled Testing phase.} In \ac{AI-DLC}, build and test conclude Construction within the same cycle, creating a risk of circular validation: tests generated by the same model from the same specification may reproduce the assumptions embedded in the implementation. Passing such tests demonstrates internal consistency rather than independent correctness, as evidenced by the gap between functional and security test performance~\cite{Zhao:2025vibe, Veracode:2025gen}. The proposed framework therefore establishes Testing as an independent phase, with its own environment, responsible role and quality gate. Validation proceeds from the locked functional specification rather than the implementation, shifting its unit from individual Bolts to the assembled User Story, where business value can be assessed.

\item \textbf{Distinct Maintenance phase with a diagnostic firewall.} \ac{AI-DLC}'s Operations phase addresses deployment and monitoring without defining how post-deployment signals are classified or assigned. The proposed framework distinguishes defects against agreed specifications, previously unspecified requirements and incorrect specifications, preventing undocumented fixes from introducing specification drift. Each signal is classified before code is accessed, after which the developer is restricted to the reported issue, relevant test evidence and implicated code fragment rather than the whole project. This diagnostic firewall limits unnecessary project-wide context and preserves specification integrity~\cite{Zhao:2025vibe}, as further discussed in \Cref{sec:spec_anchored}.

\end{itemize}

\section{Governance Mechanisms and Quality Gates}
\label{sec:governance}

% Framework doc §7. This section addresses the governance dimension and is the core contribution.

\subsection{Bolts as the Unit of Execution}
\label{sec:bolts}
A Bolt is the smallest execution iteration the framework recognises: the implementation of one task within a User Story, strictly inside the development environment and measured in hours rather than weeks. The term is adopted from \ac{AI-DLC}~\cite{AWS:2025aidlc}; what the framework adds is the sequence of obligations around it, since a short cycle without a fixed reference is a fast way to accumulate divergence.

Before any code is generated, the Tech Lead hat feeds the locked context (the approved Gherkin, the prototype and the technical specification) to the model, verifies the task list it proposes, and flags high-risk tasks. This \textbf{architectural lock} makes the technical boundary a matter of record rather than negotiation, letting tasks on either side of an interface proceed in parallel. The Bolt Master hat then assigns by capacity, giving developers the story's intent, not authority over the technical scope the lock has settled. A Developer hat pulls one task, at which moment the Bolt begins and Bolt Cycle Time is measured from. Developer and model then produce the \textbf{Consistency Factor}, a strict automated test set derived from both specifications that constrains generation rather than reporting on it afterwards; the model generates against that constraint and the developer refines the result as an active editor, not an approver of a finished proposal. A task is done when the code passes the pipeline \emph{and} the developer can explain the logic, since code no one can explain has been substituted for engineering rather than added to it. When every task is done, the story passes the Implementation Gate into staging.

The \textbf{Fix-Bolt} is the single exception: triggered by a failed Testing Gate or a verified defect, it omits task breakdown because the context already contains what that step would produce, the exact failing scenario and its evidence. It is justified by what is already known rather than being a licence to skip planning for a change that merely feels small. Its outcome is routed by risk, where the risk-proportional principle becomes operational: a low-risk patch takes the \textbf{Fast Lane} directly to the delivery pipeline without re-entering formal \ac{QA}, while a higher-risk one takes the \textbf{Secure Lane} back to staging for a Regression Bypass, in which \ac{QA} re-runs the existing suite before verifying the targeted patch.

\subsection{Spec-Anchored Continuity}
\label{sec:spec_anchored}
Spec-Anchored Continuity requires every AI-generated artefact to carry forward the context established by previously validated phases. The mechanism is a persistent versioned specification maintained alongside the codebase and split into two layers with different lifetimes. The \textbf{global layer}, the Memory Bank, holds what does not change between stories: architectural rules, technology-stack decisions, security policies and recorded workarounds for already-diagnosed defects. It is written rarely, amended deliberately, and applies to every generation on the project. The \textbf{local layer}, the Feature Specs, holds what is specific to the work in hand, the approved Gherkin acceptance criteria and the technical specification produced at the Design Gate; it is written once per story, locked at a gate, and amended only through a recorded versioned amendment. The split is what makes the mechanism usable, since a single undifferentiated context grows monotonically until it contains more irrelevant material than relevant, reintroducing the problem it was meant to solve.

The central argument is that narrowing the model's working context is a control rather than a convenience: a convenience makes a correct outcome easier to reach, whereas a control is a measure whose absence makes an incorrect outcome undetectable. Anchoring satisfies the second definition on three counts. It supplies a reference that existed before the generated artefact did, the precondition for validation being possible at all. It bounds what the model may act upon, an unbounded context being the condition under which a model produces a wide, confident and substantively wrong change, which is why whole-project context is prohibited during Maintenance diagnosis~\cite{Zhao:2025vibe}. And because both layers are versioned and every post-lock edit is a dated amendment, divergence between what was agreed and what was built becomes a detectable event rather than an unattributable drift. This is the spec-driven-development instantiation named in \Cref{tab:principles}; a recent preprint independently argues, from a multivocal review, that specification discipline rather than model capability is the binding constraint on dependability~\cite{Farrag:2026sd}. The contribution is not the idea of anchoring but the specification of where it is mandatory, which gate locks each layer, and what constitutes a legitimate amendment.

\subsection{Governance Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Three governance metrics assess the safety and flow of AI-assisted development rather than output volume, which does not necessarily translate into improved delivery~\cite{Faros:2025par}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.6cm, itemsep=0.4em]

\item \textbf{Bolt Cycle Time.} The elapsed time from a task being pulled as a Bolt to its completion. Measured at task level to isolate execution time from story-level queueing, review and gate transitions, thereby assessing continuous flow.

\item \textbf{Context Traceability Rate.} The proportion of deployed Bolts and stories whose artefacts can be traced through the locked technical and functional specifications to their originating Unit of Work. It measures whether Spec-Anchored Continuity is maintained in practice.

\item \textbf{AI Defect Escape Rate.} The proportion of production defects originating in AI-generated logic that passed the Testing Gate. It assesses gate effectiveness by identifying defects that escaped validation despite formal gate completion.

\end{itemize}

These are expressed in vocabulary continuous with established \ac{AI} governance standards rather than bespoke to this work: the \ac{NIST} \ac{AI} Risk Management Framework's Govern, Map, Measure and Manage~\cite{NIST:2023rmf} correspond respectively to this chapter's design principles, phase mapping, metrics and gates, and ISO/IEC 42001:2023 offers a complementary plan-do-check-act vocabulary for the same loop~\cite{ISO/IEC:2023ai}. This is a claim about shared language only: neither the framework nor Apex has been audited against either standard, and nothing here asserts compliance or intent to certify, only that these constructs occupy the conceptual space standards bodies are converging on.

\section{Continuous Flow in Place of Sprint Cadence}
\label{sec:work_organisation}

% Framework doc §8. The argument for replacing sprint cadence must rest on the
% measured bottleneck shift, not on preference. Moved 2026-10-02 to sit next
% to Alternatives: this section is the "why", and its "what" (the Unit of
% Work / User Story / Task hierarchy) now lives in \Cref{sec:overview},
% stated before it is needed rather than argued for only here.

The framework replaces sprints and sprint backlogs with continuous flow, the most disruptive single element of the proposal and therefore the one requiring the strongest empirical justification; the Epic, unlike the sprint, is not replaced and remains an optional grouping (\Cref{sec:overview}). Sprint cadence manages a world in which implementation is the binding constraint: a fixed-length iteration lets a quantity of implementation work be forecast, committed to and inspected at a boundary, calibrated to how long a useful amount of implementation takes. The evidence in \Cref{sec:motivation} indicates that premise no longer holds where AI assistance is heavy: developers in a randomised controlled trial were measurably slower with assistance while believing they had been faster~\cite{Becker:2025rct}, and telemetry across 1,255 teams pairs the merged-pull-request increase reported there with pull requests 154\% larger, 91\% longer review times, and no significant correlation with delivery outcomes~\cite{Faros:2025par}. The constraint moved rather than disappeared, so a cadence calibrated to the old one now obscures the new one. The structural response has two parts: execution compressed into Bolts, so the queue in front of validation refills in small increments rather than batches, and validation decoupled into its own phase, environment, hat and gate, so the constraint is scheduled and measured in its own right. Both are falsifiable: if review is not the constraint in a given setting, Bolt Cycle Time and the \ac{QA} queue will say so. The three-level work hierarchy this replaces, Unit of Work, User Story and Task, is set out in \Cref{sec:overview}.

The project management tool should provide states corresponding unambiguously to the framework's phases, without requiring a specific board configuration.

\section{Positioning Against Alternatives}
\label{sec:alternatives}

Four alternatives are considered below. Each has legitimate advantages, but the evidence motivates a different design choice.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.6cm, itemsep=0.4em]

\item \textbf{No framework at all.} This minimises process overhead and may be appropriate for short-lived prototypes with little coordination or maintenance. However, functionally correct agent output can remain insecure at scale, with security not improving alongside correctness~\cite{Zhao:2025vibe, Veracode:2025gen}. Validation therefore cannot be treated as optional process overhead.

\item \textbf{Adopt \ac{AI-DLC} unmodified.} \ac{AI-DLC} offers a coherent, commercially deployed three-phase model with less process complexity~\cite{AWS:2025aidlc}. The divergence here is limited to gaps identified in \Cref{sec:phases}: build and test are folded into Construction, while Operations provides no published mechanism for classifying post-deployment feedback. The proposed framework extends rather than replaces its useful structure.

\item \textbf{Retain Scrum or SAFe cadence and treat \ac{AI} as a faster development environment.} Existing ceremonies are familiar, well tooled and inexpensive to retain, but \Cref{sec:work_organisation} shows the premise they rest on, that implementation is the binding constraint, no longer holds under heavy AI assistance. A cadence calibrated to implementation therefore risks measuring and managing the wrong activity.

\item \textbf{Metrics-only governance, with no phase structure.} This is cheaper, flexible and consistent with evidence that no single metric captures software delivery performance~\cite{Forsgren:2021sp}. However, metrics indicate whether governance is working; they do not provide governance themselves. A dashboard can reveal rising review time or escaping defects without defining what an AI system may act upon, where validation must occur, or how post-deployment feedback is routed.

\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:proposal_summary}

The placement and governance dimensions of the problem are addressed by the artefact this chapter has specified: the six-phase model of \Cref{sec:phases} with the Strategic Alignment stage preceding it and the mapping in \Cref{tab:phase_mapping}, and \Cref{sec:governance}'s governance set of Bolts, the Consistency Factor, Spec-Anchored Continuity, five gates and three metrics. Two properties of that design are worth isolating, because a phase list and a gate list alone would not achieve them: the model is constrained at every phase to that phase's anchored specification rather than the whole project, and accountability attaches to functions rather than positions, so it survives a team of one while every failed gate routes work back to a named phase rather than discarding it.

Both are design claims and not yet evidence. The framework's constructs are an original synthesis argued from the literature but not demonstrated in use, and spec-driven development, on which Spec-Anchored Continuity rests, is characterised by its own principal source as emerging with unsettled scaling behaviour~\cite{Thoughtworks:2025SD}. \Cref{chap:apex} presents Apex, the reference implementation, and reports which constructs were implementable as specified, which were weakened or proxied, and which resisted implementation, which is the realisation dimension of the problem.

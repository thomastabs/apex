\fancychapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

The rise of \ac{GenAI} and \acp{LLM} has extended machine assistance beyond classification and retrieval towards the generation of coherent, context-aware artefacts. Within software engineering this has introduced intent-driven programming, colloquially termed \emph{Vibe Coding}, in which developers describe desired behaviour in natural language and iteratively refine what a model produces~\cite{Moore:2025vc, Zamfiroiu:2025ec}. The shift is not confined to writing code: \acp{LLM} now participate in requirements elicitation, architectural exploration, test generation, documentation and debugging, touching every phase of the \ac{SDLC}~\cite{Ahmed:2025ai, Zhang:2025ea, Hou:2025cl}. The practitioner's work changes accordingly, from manually constructing software towards specifying intent, supervising generated artefacts, and remaining accountable for architectural soundness, correctness and security.

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

The economic pull towards adoption is strong, but the evidence on outcomes is more nuanced than early enthusiasm suggested, and it is this gap between adoption and outcome that motivates the work.

Adoption is close to universal while trust is not, and the two move in opposite directions. The 2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey reports 84\% of developers using or planning to use AI tools, up from 76\%, while the proportion trusting their accuracy fell from 40\% to 29\%~\cite{StackOverflow:2025dev}; industry telemetry places daily use at around 90\%~\cite{DORA:2025ai}.

That distrust is empirically warranted, and the decisive point is that functional correctness and security do not move together. Benchmarked on real-world tasks, 61\% of the solutions produced by a leading coding agent were functionally correct but only 10.5\% secure~\cite{Zhao:2025vibe}. Across more than one hundred models, 45\% of generated samples introduced an OWASP Top 10 vulnerability, rising to 72\% for Java, and generated code carried roughly 2.74 times the vulnerabilities of human-written equivalents; critically, newer models improved at functional correctness while security performance stayed flat~\cite{Veracode:2025gen}. Capability alone does not converge on secure output.

Productivity gains are also less automatic than assumed. Early controlled evidence was favourable, reporting a task completed 55.8\% faster with an AI assistant, though it remains unpublished in a peer-reviewed venue~\cite{Peng:2023cop}. More recent work reverses the sign: in a randomised controlled trial, 16 experienced developers completing 246 tasks on their own mature repositories took 19\% longer with assistance while estimating afterwards that it had made them 20\% faster~\cite{Becker:2025rct}. The contrast is informative, since the favourable result came from a self-contained task with no existing codebase to respect and the unfavourable one from repositories the participants knew well. At organisational scale, telemetry from more than ten thousand developers across 1,255 teams associates high adoption with 98\% more pull requests merged but 91\% longer review times and no significant correlation with company-level delivery outcomes~\cite{Faros:2025par}. The bottleneck moves downstream to validation; it does not disappear.

Several of these sources are industry-reported rather than peer-reviewed and are identified as such wherever used; the argument rests on the consistency of direction across independent sources rather than on any single figure. Together they frame this dissertation: the value of AI assistance is conditional on the process surrounding it rather than a property of the model alone, and capability without governance redistributes effort rather than reducing it, degrading quality attributes that are expensive to recover later.

\section{Research Problem}
\label{sec:intro_problem}

To characterise the state of practice rigorously rather than anecdotally, an \ac{SLR} was conducted following Kitchenham's guidelines, retrieving 646 articles and synthesising the corpus around benefits, consequences, integration methodologies, stakeholder perspectives and adopting sectors; it is reported in full in \Cref{chap:slr}. Its central finding is methodological rather than technical: while the literature consistently reports meaningful benefits, it equally consistently reports that organisations lack established methodologies to shape the responsible use of \ac{AI} in professional software development~\cite{Banh:2025cf, Farhanna:2025eu, Hemmat:2025rd, Gao:2025cc}, and that proposed approaches remain fragmented across isolated tools and tasks, differ in scope and abstraction, and share no common foundation on lifecycle integration, oversight or evaluation~\cite{Ahmed:2025ai}.

Established methodologies do not close this gap. Waterfall, Scrum, Kanban and the Agile Manifesto delivered substantial improvements in adaptability and collaboration but were designed for environments in which every contributor is human~\cite{Zhang:2025ea}. They lack explicit structures for governing AI participation, bounding the context a model may act upon, assessing model reliability, and allocating responsibility over generated artefacts, and their iteration cadence assumes implementation is the binding constraint, which the evidence in \Cref{sec:motivation} contradicts.

\noindent\textbf{The research problem addressed in this work is therefore the absence of structured, repeatable guidance and standardised evaluation practices for integrating \ac{AI} and \ac{LLM}-based tools across the \ac{SDLC} while preserving human oversight, software quality and security, and organisational accountability.}

\section{Objectives and Research Questions}
\label{sec:intro_objectives}

The objective is to design, instantiate, demonstrate and evaluate a process-oriented framework addressing the problem stated above, decomposed into the research questions below. They are labelled \emph{\ac{DRQ}} to keep them distinct from the review questions RQ1 to RQ5 in \Cref{chap:slr}: RQ1 to RQ5 ask what the existing literature reports and are answered by synthesising the corpus, whereas \ac{DRQ}1 to \ac{DRQ}4 ask what this dissertation's artefacts achieve and are answered by designing, building, demonstrating and evaluating them. Both appear in \Cref{tab:rq_objective_mapping}, which is where the two could otherwise be confused.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{DRQ1:} How can AI-supported activities be explicitly mapped to \ac{SDLC} phases so that the point, purpose, and boundary of AI participation are unambiguous?
    \item \textbf{DRQ2:} Which governance mechanisms, quality gates, and human-in-the-loop practices are required to keep AI contributions traceable, validated, and accountable across the lifecycle?
    \item \textbf{DRQ3:} To what extent can the framework's governance constructs be operationalised in a working software system, and which of them resist implementation?
    \item \textbf{DRQ4:} Does applying the framework, through that implementation, provide practitioners with usable and cognitively sustainable support in a real development setting?
\end{enumerate}

DRQ1 and DRQ2 are answered by the framework design in \Cref{chap:proposal}, DRQ3 by the construction of the reference implementation in \Cref{chap:apex}, including the constructs weakened, proxied or left unimplemented, and DRQ4 by \Cref{chap:demonstration,chap:evaluation}.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This dissertation makes four contributions. The first is a \textbf{systematic synthesis of the field}: an \ac{SLR} over 646 retrieved articles organising current evidence on the benefits, consequences, integration methodologies, stakeholder perspectives and adopting sectors of \ac{AI} use in software development, with a mapping of impacts across \ac{SDLC} phases. The second is a \textbf{process framework for governed Human-AI collaboration} treating AI systems as first-class collaborators rather than passive tooling, defining lifecycle phases, phase-specific quality gates, responsibilities expressed as functions rather than job titles, governance metrics, and a persistent versioned specification anchoring generated artefacts to their originating requirements. The third is a \textbf{working reference implementation}, Apex, which operationalises every phase, gate and artefact of the framework against a real project management backend and so demonstrates that the governance constructs are implementable rather than merely describable. The fourth is an \textbf{empirical evaluation}: application of the artefact in a real project, standardised measurement of perceived usability and cognitive workload, interviews with practitioners and Agile experts, and an analytical assessment against defined criteria.

\section{Research Methodology and Document Structure}
\label{sec:intro_methodology}
\label{sec:structure}

Two complementary methodologies are employed: the \ac{SLR}, following Kitchenham's guidelines, establishes the evidence base and grounds the problem statement, and the \ac{DSRM} of Peffers et al. structures the design, construction, demonstration and evaluation of the artefact across its six activities.

\Cref{chap:methodology,chap:background} present the research methodologies and the theoretical background, \Cref{chap:slr} reports the \ac{SLR} in full and \Cref{chap:problem} formalises the research problem. \Cref{chap:proposal} derives the objectives and presents the framework, and \Cref{chap:apex} describes Apex, its reference implementation, including the design iterations driven by practitioner feedback. \Cref{chap:demonstration,chap:evaluation} report the demonstration and the evaluation, and \Cref{chap:conclusion} concludes with contributions, limitations, communication of results and future work.

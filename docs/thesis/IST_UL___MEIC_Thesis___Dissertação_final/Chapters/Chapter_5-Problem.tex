\fancychapter{Research Problem}
\label{chap:problem}
\label{sec:problem}

The review reported in \Cref{chap:slr} shows that \ac{AI} and \acp{LLM} are being adopted across sectors and throughout the \ac{SDLC}. While these tools offer substantial benefits, their effective use is hindered by the lack of established and consistent methodologies for integrating them into professional software development~\cite{Banh:2025cf, Farhanna:2025eu, Hemmat:2025rd, Gao:2025cc}.

Several AI-oriented development frameworks have been proposed, as summarised in \Cref{tab:methodologies}, but they share no common foundation for lifecycle integration, management practice or evaluation~\cite{Ahmed:2025ai}. Consequently, organisations lack standardised benchmarks and repeatable processes for assessing the effects of AI-assisted development on software quality, security and maintainability.

Established methodologies such as Waterfall, Scrum, Kanban and the Agile Manifesto were designed around human contributors and do not fully address AI-driven development~\cite{Zhang:2025ea}. AI-assisted coding can accelerate implementation while shifting effort towards requirements clarification, review, testing and maintenance. Existing approaches also provide limited guidance for governing AI participation, assessing model reliability, managing risk and assigning responsibility for AI-generated output.

Evidence discussed in \Cref{chap:intro} further suggests that faster or functionally correct code generation does not automatically improve security or delivery outcomes~\cite{Veracode:2025gen, Faros:2025par}. This indicates that the central challenge is not simply AI capability, but the process by which AI is integrated, controlled and evaluated within software development. \textbf{The core research problem addressed in this work is therefore the lack of structured methodological guidance and standardised evaluation criteria for integrating and managing \ac{AI} and \ac{LLM}-based tools across the \ac{SDLC}, while maintaining human oversight, software quality, security and organisational accountability. This gap limits organisations' ability to adopt AI-assisted software development systematically and sustainably.}

The problem has four dimensions, each traceable to the review and representing a condition that any solution must satisfy:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.6cm, itemsep=0.45em]

\item \textbf{Placement.} There is no shared account of where in the \ac{SDLC} AI participates, for what purpose, and where its participation ends. As a result, the point at which human responsibility must take over is left to individual judgement rather than fixed by the process (\Cref{tab:methodologies}).

\item \textbf{Governance.} Mechanisms for keeping AI contributions traceable to requirements, validated before progression, and attributable to an accountable person, particularly quality gates and human-in-the-loop practices, are recognised in principle but specified only ad hoc, if at all (\Cref{tab:consequences}).

\item \textbf{Realisation.} Guidance that remains purely descriptive cannot demonstrate which constructs survive implementation and which fail when enforced in a working system.

\item \textbf{Practitioner support.} AI shifts effort from code production towards review, with review times increasing as output grows~\cite{Faros:2025par} and over-reliance recurring as a risk (\Cref{tab:consequences}). Guidance that is methodologically sound but too burdensome to apply therefore does not resolve the problem.

\end{itemize}

The first two dimensions define what structured guidance must contain; the latter two concern whether that guidance can be implemented and sustained in practice.

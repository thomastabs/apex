\fancychapter{Research Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter presents the two research methodologies used in this dissertation, the \ac{SLR} and the \ac{DSRM}, and maps the activities of the second onto the chapters of this document.

\section{Systematic Literature Review (SLR)} 

The review follows Kitchenham's guidelines, which describe a systematic review as “a means of evaluating and interpreting all available research relevant to a particular research question, topic area, or phenomenon of interest” conducted through “a trustworthy, rigorous, and auditable methodology.” The guidelines prescribe three phases: \textbf{planning}, which defines the motivation, research questions, selection criteria and protocol; \textbf{conducting}, which applies the protocol to identify and select studies; and \textbf{reporting}, which synthesises the findings.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{./Images/phases.png}
    \caption{Three phases of the Systematic Literature Review}
    \label{fig:slr_phases}
\end{figure}

\Cref{fig:slr_phases} illustrates those phases and their activities. Filtering ran over several iterations: an initial screening of retrieved articles, an abstract-reading pass to assess relevance, a filter applying Kitchenham's criteria to introductions and conclusions, and a full-text review determining the retained set. The methodology was selected because it enables a structured and unbiased collection of existing research on AI-supported software development and its associated management practices.

\section{Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM)}

The \ac{DSRM} concerns the development of IT artefacts, such as models, methods or frameworks, addressing identified organisational or technological problems. Unlike purely observational research it emphasises constructing solutions and evaluating their usefulness. Proposed by Peffers et al., it follows a six-step process supporting systematic design, clear contributions and transferable knowledge, which makes it suitable for research proposing process-oriented solutions. The steps are illustrated in \Cref{fig:dsrm} and described below.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{./Images/dsrm.png}
    \caption{Phases of the Design Science Research Methodology}
    \label{fig:dsrm}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Problem identification and motivation} addresses the lack of structured guidance, standardised methodologies and evaluation practices for integrating \ac{AI} into software development while preserving quality, accountability and human oversight; the problem was identified through the \ac{SLR}, which found fragmented practices and unclear human-AI role definitions across the \ac{SDLC}. \textbf{Definition of objectives} derives from that problem objectives aimed at guiding organisations towards effective, secure and responsible adoption through appropriate processes and governance mechanisms. \textbf{Design and development} takes the review findings as input and produces the framework, defining phases, practices, hats and decision points, together with Apex as its reference implementation. \textbf{Demonstration} applies the artefact to a real project, exercising the phases end to end and producing documented artefacts, decision rationales and observations recorded against criteria fixed in advance. \textbf{Evaluation} separates the two artefacts it assesses: the framework through interviews with practitioners and Agile experts and an analytical assessment against pre-stated criteria, the instantiation through a task-based study measuring perceived usability and cognitive workload with standardised instruments; that separation is argued in \Cref{chap:evaluation}, since a usability result for the tool is not evidence that the method is sound. \textbf{Communication} disseminates results through a scientific article reporting the \ac{SLR}, the extended abstract accompanying this dissertation, and the defence.

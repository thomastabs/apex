\fancychapter{Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

This chapter reports the Evaluation activity of the \ac{DSRM}. Following Pries-Heje et al.'s distinction between \emph{when} and \emph{how} an artefact is evaluated~\cite{J.Pries-Heje:2008SD}, the evaluation carried out here is \emph{ex post}, since it assesses the artefact after construction and on the basis of its actual use, and \emph{naturalistic}, since it takes place in a real organisational setting with real users rather than in a laboratory.

\ac{SUS} and \ac{NASA-TLX} measure Apex, not the framework. Both are administered to someone who has spent a session operating a user interface, and neither contains an item about phase decomposition, gate placement, or accountability for generated artefacts. A favourable \ac{SUS} score therefore establishes that the tool is usable and nothing at all about whether the method it implements is sound; an unfavourable one, correspondingly, is not evidence against the method. That is a design decision rather than a defect: the framework's own validity is carried by the practitioner and expert interviews (\Cref{sec:interviews}), the analytical assessment against criteria fixed in advance (\Cref{sec:analytical}), and the demonstration (\Cref{chap:demonstration}), each of which reaches the method directly.

One instrument spans both artefacts, and the split is handled rather than ignored: two items of the author-designed \ac{UX} questionnaire ask about the process rather than the product, and are analysed apart from the fifteen instantiation-scoped ones, as \Cref{sec:apex_ux} sets out.

\section{Participants}
\label{sec:eval_participants}

Participants were recruited through the author's own professional and personal connections. There was no open call, no advertisement and no mailing list, and the number of people approached was not recorded, so no response rate can be reported and none is claimed. Thirteen sessions were completed, coded P0 to P12; all thirteen returned all four instruments and no participant withdrew. The pre-registered target was twelve; the achieved sample is thirteen, and the sample is not drawn from a single organisation but from one person's professional and personal network, which is a different limit rather than a weaker one.

The sample comprised thirteen participants and was predominantly junior. Three were students with no professional experience, three had under one year, and seven had one to three years; none exceeded three years. By role, six were students, four developers, one an \ac{AI} engineer, and two worked outside software development. Exposure to AI coding assistants ranged from daily use (four participants) to no prior use (one), with the remainder distributed across weekly, monthly and occasional use. Familiarity with the session's supporting tools was limited: nine participants had no prior exposure to Gherkin or \ac{BDD}, and ten reported no familiarity with Taiga. Nine participants selected English as their session language and four Portuguese; questionnaire language differed for two participants, as discussed in \Cref{sec:threats}. The sample therefore supports only bounded claims. Thirteen respondents meets the twelve at which a \ac{SUS} mean is generally treated as stable, so the mean is reported alongside its distribution and individual scores, but the convenience sample remains junior-skewed and drawn from a single network; the resulting limitations are discussed in \Cref{sec:threats}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.6cm, itemsep=0.3em, topsep=0.2em]

\item \textbf{Consent.} All participants provided informed consent before data collection through separate choices for participation, anonymised quotation and follow-up contact. All thirteen consented to participate; eleven consented to quotation and eleven to follow-up contact, the two sets overlapping at nine, with quotations restricted accordingly. The consent form is reproduced in \Cref{chapter:appendixB}. No institutional ethics submission was required under the guidance obtained for this study.

\item \textbf{Data collection.} Sessions and interviews were not audio, video or screen-recorded. Collected data comprised questionnaire responses, submission timestamps and contemporaneous interview notes written up immediately after each interview. The limitations of note-based rather than transcript-based analysis are addressed in \Cref{sec:threats}.

\item \textbf{Anonymisation and retention.} Participants are identified only as P0-P12; names, employers and identifying project details were not retained with responses. Only wording recorded verbatim is presented as quotation, with other interview material explicitly paraphrased. Anonymised responses and interview notes are retained with researcher-only access as described in \Cref{sec:appB_consent}, permitting the reported aggregates to be recomputed.

\end{itemize}

\section{Instruments and Procedure}
\label{sec:eval_instruments_procedure}

The usability study was administered entirely unmoderated. Participants worked independently, at their own pace, from a fixed written task script and completed the questionnaires online without the researcher present. A small moderated think-aloud arm was considered but deliberately removed. Without recordings, observation would have produced only facilitator notes, and because the author designed both artefacts under evaluation, facilitation would also have introduced a direct risk of steering, hinting or reacting to participants. The trade-off is the absence of measured task completion, time on task, assist counts and observational evidence of where participants struggled, which is discussed in \Cref{sec:threats}.

\subsection{System Usability Scale}
\label{sec:sus}

The \ac{SUS}~\cite{Brooke:1996SUS} is a ten-item, five-point questionnaire administered after task completion. Participants using Portuguese received the validated European Portuguese version~\cite{Martins:2015SUS}; those using English received the original wording, both reproduced in \Cref{sec:appB_sus}. The two language groups are reported separately rather than pooled.

Scoring follows the standard procedure: odd-numbered items contribute the response minus one, even-numbered items contribute five minus the response, and the total is multiplied by 2.5 to produce a score from 0 to 100. The result is not a percentage and is therefore interpreted using published reference scales rather than in isolation.

Two interpretations are reported. Bangor et al. associate score ranges with qualitative labels, with reference points including \emph{Poor} at 35.7, \emph{OK} at 50.9, \emph{Good} at 71.4 and \emph{Excellent} at 85.5~\cite{Bangor:2009SUS}. Sauro and Lewis instead map scores to percentile ranks and letter grades against their corpus, where the commonly cited mean of approximately 68 corresponds to the fiftieth percentile rather than to a pass threshold~\cite{Sauro:2016QUANT}. Both are reported in \Cref{sec:eval_results}.

The Lewis and Sauro two-factor decomposition is also reported: \textbf{Usability} from the eight items other than 4 and 10, and \textbf{Learnability} from items 4 and 10~\cite{Lewis:2009SUS}. These dimensions are useful because a tool may be difficult to learn while remaining usable once learned, or perform poorly on both.

The Portuguese results are interpreted cautiously. The validation study reported acceptable construct validity against the Post-Study System Usability Questionnaire ($r = 0.70$), but weak inter-rater reliability (ICC $=0.36$, 95\% CI $0.01$--$0.63$) and 76.67\% agreement, below the authors' 80\% threshold~\cite{Martins:2015SUS}. Since only three participants completed the Portuguese version here, those results are reported separately and treated as indicative.

\subsection{NASA Task Load Index}
\label{sec:tlx}

The \ac{NASA-TLX}~\cite{Hart:1988TLX} measures perceived workload across mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort and frustration. This study uses the unweighted Raw \ac{NASA-TLX}~\cite{Hart:2006TLX}, administered after each marked task rather than once at the end of the session, thereby producing a workload profile by lifecycle phase.

The Raw variant was chosen because per-task administration would otherwise require ninety pairwise weighting comparisons per participant. More importantly, Hart's review of 550 studies found no consistent sensitivity advantage for the weighted form across direct comparisons~\cite{Hart:2006TLX}. The six subscale scores, which are the primary results in this study, are identical under both variants; weighting affects only the aggregate.

Two departures from the published instrument were made. Responses used a 0--10 linear scale rather than the original twenty-interval line and were rescaled to 0--100 by multiplying by ten. This simplifies unmoderated administration but reduces resolution, so small differences are not interpreted as meaningful and the values are not treated as directly comparable with studies using the original response scale. The reversed performance scale was also placed last rather than fourth to reduce the risk of inversion errors during an unmoderated session. Neither change alters the Raw \ac{NASA-TLX} computation. Repeated administration may introduce anchoring and fatigue, particularly on later tasks. These risks are discussed in \Cref{sec:threats} and weighed against the benefit of obtaining a phase-specific workload profile. Both instruments were applied to one fixed task set, identical in wording and order for all participants. Nine tasks carried a single password-reset Unit of Work from requirement to deployed increment:

\subsection{Apex UX Questionnaire}
\label{sec:apex_ux}

An eighteen-item questionnaire was developed for this evaluation to capture aspects not addressed by \ac{SUS} or Raw \ac{NASA-TLX}: trust in AI-generated content, perceptions of phase gates, and whether reviewing generated output felt cheaper than producing it. The items were grouped into five constructs: process understanding, interaction with AI-generated content, control and reversibility, feedback and errors, and fit to real work. Because the questionnaire is not validated, results are reported item by item rather than as composite or subscale scores. It was administered after \ac{SUS} and is reproduced in \Cref{chapter:appendixB}. Two items in the fifth construct concern the framework rather than Apex itself, asking whether the artefacts and process fit real work. Although presented on the same questionnaire for participant convenience, they are analysed separately under the framework evidence in \Cref{tab:eval_instruments}. Two cross-item relationships were defined in advance:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.6cm, itemsep=0.3em, topsep=0.2em]

\item \textbf{Trust and review effort.} Item B4 asks whether participants trusted generated content enough to use it in a real project, while B2 asks whether reviewing it required less effort than writing it. Read together, they provide a trust-calibration indicator: high trust with low review effort may suggest acceptance without sufficient scrutiny, whereas high trust with substantial review effort is more consistent with confidence formed through checking. This pair provides the closest available proxy, in an unmoderated self-report design, for whether generated output was critically reviewed rather than accepted wholesale.

\item \textbf{Gate comprehension.} Item A4, \emph{when the tool refused to let me continue, I understood why}, was originally intended to be interpreted alongside task~8 \ac{NASA-TLX} Frustration and free-text responses. When task~8 was redesigned from a deliberate refusal test into a successful deployment flow (\Cref{sec:tlx}), A4 was removed from both language versions on 30 August 2026. Seventeen of the original eighteen items were therefore administered. A4 represents no missing data, since it was absent for all participants; however, its removal eliminated the study's only direct measure of whether a phase gate was perceived as a guardrail or an obstruction, a limitation carried into \Cref{sec:threats}.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Semi-Structured Interviews}
\label{sec:interviews}

The interviews used a population separate from the unmoderated task-based study in \Cref{sec:eval_participants}. Practitioners and an Agile/Scrum expert were recruited independently because the interview guides in \Cref{chapter:appendixB} address framework practicality, adoption cost and alignment with established delivery methods rather than task-level usability. The Agile-expert guide therefore does not require prior use of Apex. Themes were derived from the contemporaneous notes described in \Cref{sec:eval_participants}: an initial codebook was developed from the first interview in each group and extended as additional themes emerged. Session duration was not recorded systematically. Interviewees are identified only by code: T1--T5 for practitioners and E1 for the Agile/Scrum expert. These codes are intentionally separate from the P-codes used in the unmoderated study because the two populations are disjoint.

Five practitioners and one Agile expert had been interviewed at the time of writing. This is at or below the lower bound of the intended recruitment targets, so the results should be read as a small set of attributable, information-rich interviews rather than a saturated qualitative sample. T3 was interviewed twice after substantial framework revision. T1 and T5 are not analysed here because their earlier feedback directly informed design changes and is reported in \Cref{sec:apex_history}; the evidence in this section therefore comes from T2, T3, T4 and E1.

\paragraph{Practitioners.} Three themes emerged:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.6cm, itemsep=0.3em, topsep=0.2em]

\item \textbf{Specification as durable project memory.} T2 endorsed the idea of the locked specification as a persistent source of project knowledge, combining guidelines, requirements, plans and tests. The same practitioner also warned that implicit assumptions are difficult to make fully explicit.

\item \textbf{Versioning and non-linearity.} T4 argued that specification history should remain inspectable and editable through semantic versioning, and independently raised the risk that a strictly linear phase sequence could reproduce waterfall-style rigidity. The latter concern is notable because it was also anticipated in the Agile-expert interview guide, giving it support from two independent directions.

\item \textbf{Interface and framing issues.} T3's first interview raised two narrower concerns: richer project-management context in AI-generated requirements and clearer on-screen state during UI review. T3's second interview produced the strongest challenge to the framework and is reported in \Cref{sec:apex_history}, where its traceable design consequences are discussed.

\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Agile/Scrum expert.}
The expert interview followed a live framework presentation and Apex demonstration. The strongest positive reaction concerned the value of executable instantiation: the expert found the framework easier to interpret when roles, phases and gates were demonstrated in a working tool rather than described only in prose. The flexible use of hats rather than fixed named roles, Mob Elaboration, and the governance metrics were also identified as points of difference from more rigid organisational methodologies. Two limitations were also raised. First, the expert experienced first-contact navigation confusion, consistent with the orientation difficulties identified elsewhere in the evaluation (\Cref{sec:eval_discussion}). Second, the expert described an organisational setting in which AI use is centrally restricted and metered, but with no dedicated governance role. This prompted a broader question about whether AI participation produces a net cost or benefit. The framework's metrics in \Cref{sec:metrics} address governance effects, but do not yet operationalise token or usage cost specifically; this is carried forward in \Cref{chap:conclusion}.

\paragraph{Read across both groups.}
The strongest convergent finding is the concern over excessive linearity: it was raised unprompted by a practitioner and independently anticipated in the expert guide. The strongest divergence concerns the specification-as-source-of-truth idea: T2 endorsed it, while the sharpest criticism of the same construct is reported separately in \Cref{sec:apex_history}. The evidence is therefore mixed rather than uniformly favourable.


\subsection{Analytical Assessment}
\label{sec:analytical}
The framework is additionally assessed against five criteria fixed before the results were seen rather than derived from them. \emph{Clarity} asks whether each construct is defined precisely enough to be applied without its author present. \emph{Completeness} asks whether the framework covers the lifecycle it claims to cover, including its own defined measurements. \emph{Alignment with \ac{SDLC} phases} asks whether the phase decomposition maps onto recognised lifecycle stages without merging, splitting or omitting one. \emph{Support for human-in-the-loop practice} asks whether the framework makes human review of generated output structurally necessary rather than merely advisable. \emph{Governance of AI-enabled activity} asks whether AI contributions are made traceable, attributable and measurable by mechanism rather than by discipline.

Each is rated on a three-level ordinal scale. \emph{Met} means the criterion is satisfied and evidence exists that it is. \emph{Partially met} means it is satisfied in part and the shortfall is identifiable rather than merely suspected. \emph{Not met} means it is not satisfied. No finer scale is used, because the evidence available cannot distinguish more than three levels.

Each rating is justified against evidence that exists independently of the author's opinion of his own design: the construct-to-mechanism mapping in \Cref{tab:apex_mapping}, whose negative rows are as usable here as its positive ones; the interviews in \Cref{sec:interviews}; and incidents recorded during the study sessions themselves. Where the only available support for a rating would be the author's own assessment, the rating is lowered rather than argued, on the principle that a criterion which cannot be evidenced is not distinguishable from one that is not met. The ratings are reported in \Cref{tab:analytical}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:eval_results}

\subsection{System Usability Scale}
\label{sec:results_sus}

Thirteen participants returned complete \ac{SUS} responses, with all individual scores reported in \Cref{tab:sus_scores} (\Cref{chapter:appendixB}). Given the small sample, the distribution is reported alongside the mean rather than relying on a single aggregate.

The overall mean was \textbf{56.73} (SD 18.50, median 60, range 15--80), below the published corpus mean of approximately 68. On the curved grading scale this corresponds to \textbf{grade D}, in the \textbf{15th--34th percentile band}; on the adjective scale it lies between \emph{OK} at \textbf{50.9} and \emph{Good} at \textbf{71.4}, closer to the former. The language groups are reported separately: the ten English responses averaged 60.50 (SD 19.18, median 63.75), also grade D, whereas the three Portuguese responses averaged 44.17 (SD 9.46, median 40), corresponding to \textbf{grade F} in the \textbf{0th--14th percentile band}. The Portuguese subgroup is too small to support broader claims and is interpreted cautiously (\Cref{sec:sus,sec:threats}).

The distribution is uneven: six participants scored above 60, two exactly 60 and five below, with four at or above 70. P6 recorded the lowest score, \textbf{15}, below Bangor's \emph{Awful} anchor at \textbf{20.3} and close to \emph{Worst imaginable} at \textbf{12.5}. This value is retained, but interpreted alongside the participant's Raw \ac{NASA-TLX} responses, where 35 of 36 ratings were identical, suggesting a constant-response pattern. With thirteen participants there is neither sufficient justification to exclude the case nor enough statistical capacity to treat it as negligible.

The subscales show a clearer pattern. Usability averaged \textbf{59.86}, while Learnability averaged \textbf{44.23}. Learnability also showed much greater dispersion, ranging from \textbf{0 to 100}: P2 scored \textbf{0}, maximally agreeing that technical support and substantial prior learning would be needed, while P11 scored \textbf{100}, disagreeing with both as strongly as the scale permits. Usability ranged from \textbf{12.50 to 87.50}. This suggests that the stronger difficulty lies in initial learning and orientation rather than in continued use once the system is understood, a point taken up in \Cref{sec:eval_discussion}.

The pre-defined trust-calibration pair did not diverge at the median. B2, reviewing requiring less effort than writing, and B4, trust sufficient for real use, both had a median of \textbf{4}, with no participant below \textbf{3} on either item. This is consistent with calibrated trust at the aggregate level, but P7's free text described the opposite pattern directly: ``I did not review in great detail and I trusted it completely''. Because the sessions were unobserved, the study cannot determine how common that behaviour was; this limitation is carried into \Cref{sec:threats}.

The framework-scoped items show a more nuanced result. E3, whether the process effort is worth its output, received no disagreement: \textbf{0} responses at 1 or 2, \textbf{2} neutral, \textbf{5} at 4 and \textbf{6} at 5, giving a median of \textbf{4}. E2, whether the process fits the participant's team, was the weakest-supported of the seventeen items: \textbf{3} marked \emph{N/A}, none strongly agreed, \textbf{2} disagreed, \textbf{2} were neutral and \textbf{6} agreed, also yielding a median of \textbf{4}. The same three participants marked \emph{N/A} on E1, so those missing values are related rather than independent; all three answered E3. The main distinction is therefore between perceived value and organisational fit: participants were more willing to say the process was worth its cost than that it matched how their own team works.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Construct validity.} Every instrument here measures perception, and none measures delivered software quality. \ac{SUS} and Raw \ac{NASA-TLX} are standardised and comparable against published corpora, which is the mitigation that does apply; the \ac{UX} questionnaire's construct-validity limit is that it is author-written and unvalidated, addressed by treating it as such (\Cref{sec:apex_ux}). Two departures already described (\Cref{sec:tlx}) reduce what the workload instrument can resolve: the 0 to 10 response scale, and the anchoring and fatigue risk of six repeated administrations. One respondent's data is consistent with exactly that: thirty-five of P6's thirty-six workload ratings are the same number. No mitigation was available for either.

The sharpest construct gap concerns phase gates: the item A4/task-8 cross-read described in \Cref{sec:apex_ux}, this study's only planned evidence on whether a gate reads as a guardrail or as obstruction, did not survive task 8's redesign away from a refusal test (\Cref{sec:tlx}). The gap is therefore worse than the absence of an observer check: there was no guaranteed refusal trigger for any of the thirteen participants, so gate comprehension is unmeasured rather than weakly measured, the framework's most distinctive mechanism the one this evaluation has least evidence about.

\paragraph{Internal validity.} The author designed both artefacts, wrote the task script and the seventeen \ac{UX} items, built the seeded demonstration project, and conducted the interviews. The unmoderated design removes that conflict from the sessions structurally (\Cref{sec:eval_instruments_procedure}), the strongest mitigation available, but removes nothing from the instruments themselves. Two incidents make the residue concrete rather than hypothetical. A configuration defect let an interface-language setting leak between projects, so a nominally English session was answered by the model in Portuguese, and it was noticed only because the author recognised the symptom in a participant's free text and root-caused it himself. A second defect, in which Phase 5 read a stale repository-context file as current and reported at high confidence, was found during the first session and was still present for every subsequent participant. In both cases the person best placed to detect and interpret the anomaly was the artefact's designer.

The same window produced a second internal threat: the system was not frozen. One defect fix was deployed during data collection and one configuration change followed it, so participants did not all use a byte-identical build. Against that, the project environment was reset to an identical seeded state before every session (\Cref{sec:tlx}).

\paragraph{External validity.} The sample (\Cref{sec:eval_participants}) is a convenience sample from one person's professional network, skewed junior, with no open call and no recorded count of those approached. It supports a description of how these thirteen people experienced the tool and no claim about experienced practitioners, teams or organisations. The pre-registered concern about a single organisational setting turns out to apply in a different place than expected: the participants are not from one organisation, but the \emph{task} environment is a single seeded project on one project-management tool, which ten of the thirteen had never used, so unfamiliarity with that board is confounded with unfamiliarity with Apex throughout. The observation window is one session of roughly an hour, which precludes any claim about long-term maintainability, adoption or organisational change; one participant makes the point himself, expecting that ``with regular use, I believe the user would become very familiar with the interface'' (P12), which is a hypothesis this design cannot test. The Portuguese figures rest on three respondents administered an instrument whose own validation reports weak inter-rater agreement, and are reported as indicative only.

\paragraph{Conclusion validity.} Thirteen participants spread over five levels of prior AI exposure cannot support inferential statistics or subgroup comparison, and none is computed. Two administration artefacts show what an unmoderated design cannot catch as it happens: one participant declared Portuguese as the session language and then completed every instrument in English, and another declared English but submitted the Portuguese consent form. Neither was correctable at the time because nobody was present to correct it.

\paragraph{The cost of recording nothing.} Two threats follow from the decision not to observe or record, and both are consequences of a deliberate design rather than oversights. First, there is no observational evidence of any kind: no measured completion rate, no time on task beyond a form-submission timestamp that cannot distinguish task difficulty from an interruption, no assist or error counts, and no independent check on comprehension anywhere. This leaves the perception-versus-quality threat standing rather than partly answered, and it is why the trust-calibration pair in \Cref{sec:results_ux} can report that trust and review effort did not diverge without being able to say whether any individual accepted output unread. Second, the interview analysis rests on contemporaneous written notes rather than transcripts (\Cref{sec:eval_participants}), and the selection was made by the person who designed the artefact under discussion; \Cref{sec:interviews} names which interviewee raised which point precisely so that the selection is at least attributable.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:eval_discussion}

The instruments do not converge on a single narrative; the most informative findings lie in where they agree for different reasons and where they diverge.

The first apparent tension is between \ac{SUS} and the \ac{UX} questionnaire. The overall \ac{SUS} mean was \textbf{56.73} (grade D), while several mechanism-level \ac{UX} items reached medians of \textbf{5}, including the ability to reject generated output, undo a step, and tolerate generation time. These results concern different levels: \ac{SUS} evaluates the system as a whole, whereas the \ac{UX} items evaluate individual mechanisms. Free-text responses reinforce this distinction: \textbf{9 of 13} participants identified density, orientation or navigation as the main source of confusion, rather than the AI mechanisms themselves. The \ac{SUS} subscales point in the same direction, with Learnability at \textbf{44.23} compared with Usability at \textbf{59.86}. The combined evidence therefore suggests that the mechanisms are individually understandable, while the interface that assembles them imposes substantial first-contact cost. This is a finding about Apex rather than the framework itself (\Cref{sec:eval_strategy}).

A second cross-read concerns error visibility. D1, on understanding what had happened when something went wrong, was the weakest closed \ac{UX} item. Independently, Design and Packs were the two heaviest workload tasks, and Packs produced the highest frustration score at \textbf{26.2} despite being predicted to impose only moderate load (\Cref{sec:results_tlx}). P8's free text connects the two findings directly: an error was insufficiently surfaced, allowing subsequent stages to be completed on an incorrect assumption. The weakness in D1 therefore reflects more than message wording; poor error legibility can allow a formally successful workflow to continue from a faulty state.

The clearest convergence occurs around deployment. Task~8 recorded the highest Performance value of any task at \textbf{21.5}, meaning participants rated their deployment success furthest from \emph{Perfect}. Four participants also commented on Phase~5 pre-flight reasoning, while an independently recorded defect showed that the same mechanism read stale repository context as current and reported its verdict with high confidence (\Cref{sec:threats}). Questionnaire data, free text and an observed implementation defect therefore converge on the same governance mechanism, supporting the \emph{partially met} rating in \Cref{tab:analytical}.

Trust remains unresolved. B2, on review effort, and B4, on trust sufficient for real use, both had a median of \textbf{4}, with no aggregate divergence. However, one participant explicitly reported trusting the generated output completely and not reviewing it in detail. Because the sessions were unobserved, the study cannot determine whether this behaviour was exceptional or common. The instrument therefore found no evidence of systematic trust miscalibration, but the design was not capable of ruling it out.

Finally, the tool and the framework received different forms of evaluation. Apex scored below average on usability, while practitioner and expert interviews were broadly positive about the framework, including its specification-centred approach and flexible hat structure (\Cref{sec:interviews}). This difference is not contradictory: the usability instruments evaluate the implementation, whereas the interviews address the methodology. As established in \Cref{sec:eval_strategy}, weaknesses in the interface do not by themselves constitute evidence against the framework.

\subsection{Answering the Research Questions}
\label{sec:eval_rq_answers}

\paragraph{DRQ1, how can AI-supported activities be mapped to \ac{SDLC} phases so that the point, purpose and boundary of AI participation are unambiguous?}
The framework answers this at phase granularity through \Cref{tab:phase_mapping}: each phase defines the permitted AI contribution, required output, accountable hat and exit control. The decomposition survived instantiation without any phase being merged, split or omitted, and the alignment criterion is rated \emph{met} in \Cref{tab:analytical}. An independent expert also recognised the phase and hat structure as distinct from more rigid organisational methodologies (\Cref{sec:interviews}). The qualification is clarity: phase boundaries are explicit, but at least one construct depended on an unstated precondition that only implementation exposed.

\paragraph{DRQ2, which governance mechanisms, quality gates and human-in-the-loop practices keep AI contributions traceable, validated and accountable?}
The strongest mechanisms are specification-based gates, stable identifiers and server-side reconciliation of generated claims (\Cref{sec:governance,sec:gates,sec:apex_qa}). These make artefacts traceable to requirements and prevent generation alone from satisfying a phase. Human-in-the-loop support is therefore rated \emph{met} structurally, and participants independently reported that generated output could be distinguished, rejected and revised (\Cref{tab:analytical}, \Cref{tab:ux_items}). Accountability remains weaker and is rated \emph{partially met}: AI-attributable defects can only be observed through a proxy, and one governance surface was found reporting confidently from stale evidence. The framework therefore makes AI contributions traceable and reviewable, but not yet fully measurable in terms of downstream consequences.

\paragraph{DRQ3, to what extent can those constructs be operationalised in a working system, and which resist it?}
Of the twelve constructs mapped in \Cref{tab:apex_mapping}, \textbf{ten are realised as specified}, \textbf{one is realised only as a proxy}, and \textbf{one is partially realised}. The exceptions are analytically important. Bolt Cycle Time was initially implemented using the wrong proxy and corrected only by comparison with its written definition (\Cref{sec:apex_framework_mapping}); spec-drift detection exposed an unstated assumption that specifications are scoped per story rather than per project.

The demonstration shows that operationalisation also survives real use. Across the deployed project, Context Traceability resolved for \textbf{50 of 54 stories}, and one shared-component conformance defect was detected consistently across \textbf{nine stories} (\Cref{sec:results_demonstration}). However, a realised construct is not necessarily a reliable one: the Testing Gate was shown passable by a suite that no longer measured the current system, and stale-context governance failures recurred. Operationalisation is therefore substantial but incomplete in reliability.

\paragraph{DRQ4, does the framework, through that implementation, give practitioners usable and cognitively sustainable support in a real setting?}
The evidence is mixed but internally consistent. Cognitive workload was comparatively low: the grand Raw \ac{NASA-TLX} aggregate was \textbf{21.5/100}, frustration averaged \textbf{16.7}, and the heaviest task scored \textbf{30.4}. Workload was concentrated in mental demand and in phases requiring substantial review of generated material.

Usability was weaker. The overall \ac{SUS} mean was \textbf{56.73}, grade D, with one participant scoring \textbf{15}; the Portuguese subgroup averaged \textbf{44.17}, grade F. Error legibility was the weakest \ac{UX} area, and \textbf{9 of 13} participants identified density, orientation or navigation as the main source of confusion. The combined interpretation is that Apex imposes relatively modest workload once users understand the workflow, but does not support initial orientation well enough. These are findings about the instantiation, not the framework itself, and remain bounded by the one-session evaluation and sample limitations in \Cref{sec:threats}. The nine-day demonstration provides complementary evidence of sustained use, but also exposed the same governance surfaces as vulnerable to stale or misleading evidence (\Cref{sec:results_demonstration}).

For future work, usability and visual simplicity should be treated as non-negotiable design requirements. Because Apex is the main interface through which practitioners encounter the framework, visual complexity or poor orientation can be mistaken for methodological difficulty, especially by first-time users. Improving first-contact clarity, navigation and visual hierarchy is therefore necessary not only for the interface, but for practical adoption of the framework itself.
